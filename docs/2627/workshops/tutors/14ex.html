In this week’s workshop you will work with a real dataset from He (2020) containing instructor star ratings and difficulty ratings given by students on RateMyProfessor.com.

You can access the dataset at https://uoepsy.github.io/data/ratemyprof_stars.csv. Before you start answering any questions, read the data into R.

Using a 5% significance level, you will answer the following research question:

When students rate instructors as higher difficulty, do they also tend to give those instructors lower star ratings?

Question 1

Visualise the association between how difficult the instructor is rated (student_difficult and the star rating given (student_star). Write a brief summary of what you observe.

TipHints
  • Create a bar plot with student_difficult on the x-axis and the count of ratings on the y-axis, filled by student_star.
  • You will need to convert student_star into a factor for the fill aesthetic to work correctly. You can do this directly into aes() as follows aes(..., fill = factor(variable)).

Solution 1. 

library(tidyverse)
rmp_data <- read_csv('https://uoepsy.github.io/data/ratemyprof_stars.csv')

rmp_data |>
  mutate(student_star = factor(student_star)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = student_difficult, fill = student_star)) +
  geom_bar() +
  labs(x = "Difficulty Rating", fill = "Star Rating")

Converting student_star into a factor inside of the aes() function leads to the same plot.

rmp_data |>
  ggplot(aes(x = student_difficult, fill = factor(student_star))) +
  geom_bar() +
  labs(x = "Difficulty Rating", fill = "Star Rating")

As the difficulty rating increases, the proportion of lower star ratings (e.g., 1 or 2 stars) tends to increase, while the proportion of higher star ratings (e.g., 4 or 5 stars) tends to decrease. This suggests a negative association between perceived difficulty and star rating.

Question 2

Before running any test, it’s important to choose the correct statistical test and define your hypotheses.

  1. What type of correlation is appropriate here and why?
  2. Define the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)) and alternative hypothesis (\(H_1\)) for your research question, using both plain English and mathematical symbols.
TipHints
  1. Think about data types. What type of data are needed for Pearson’s correlation, and what is the type of your variables?
  2. Use the symbol \(\rho_S\) to represent the Spearman population correlation coefficient.

Solution 2.

  1. The variable student_difficult is ordinal (Likert scale 1-5), not truly continuous interval/ratio data. Likert data often violate Pearson’s assumptions of normality and linearity. Spearman’s correlation is used in the presence of ordinal data, making it the appropriate choice here.

  2. Hypotheses:

    • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): There is no association between instructor difficulty rating and star rating in the population.
      In symbols: \(H_0\): \(\rho_S = 0\)
    • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): There is an association between instructor difficulty rating and star rating in the population.
      In symbols: \(H_1\): \(\rho_S \neq 0\)

Question 3

Test your hypotheses using the cor.test() function in R.

TipHints

  • Use the argument method = ... to compute the correct correlation type.

  • You might see the warning message “Cannot compute exact p-value with ties”. This is normal for Likert data, as multiple participants can give the same rating, creating “tied ranks”. This is not an error, so you shouldn’t worry about it.

Solution 3. 

cor.test(rmp_data$student_difficult, rmp_data$student_star, method = "spearman")

Question 5

Let’s now practice how to report the results of a correlaiton test using APA style.

Write up a few sentences that summarise your correlation test results. Also include the test results following APA style:

  • Spearman’s ranked correlation = [correlation coefficient], \(p\) = [p-value]
TipHints

This is the write-up from the lecture slides as an example:

We wanted to find out whether students from higher-income families tend to achieve higher test scores than students from lower-income families. However, a Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that our income variable deviated significantly from a normal distribution (\(W\) = 1, \(p\) < .001). We therefore used Spearman’s ranked correlation instead of Pearson’s \(r\) to test the correlation between income and test score.

We found a weak positive correlation (Spearman’s ranked correlation = .2, \(p\) < .001). This result suggests that higher family income is significantly associated with higher test scores, and lower family income is associated with lower test scores.

Solution 5.

We investigated whether students’ ratings of instructor difficulty were significantly associated with star ratings. The variable types (ordinal and numerical) indicated that Pearson’s correlation was not appropriate, so a Spearman’s correlation test was performed. There was a significant negative correlation between difficulty rating and star rating, which was weak-to-moderate in magnitude (Spearman’s ranked correlation = -.42, \(p\) < .001). This suggests that as instructors are rated as more difficult, they tend to receive lower star ratings.
