This week, we’re going to draw on the ideas we covered over the last few weeks and go step-by-step through the process of performing a ‘null hypothesis significance test’.

We start with a research question:

Research Question: Are first year undergraduate students more optimistic than adults in general?
Operationalisation: We surveyed students on a first year undergraduate statistics course (that’s you!), and asked them to indicate their level of pessimism/optimism on a sliding scale from -100 (pessimist) to +100 (optimist). Previous research using this measure suggests that the overall adult population has an average score of 35.
Data: https://uoepsy.github.io/data/dapr1_2526_survey.csv

Question 2

In future, we’re going to learn and use functions in R that do all the steps of NHST for us, but for this week we’re going to break down the process, so we’re going to need all the different parts.

We’re going to need:

  1. the size of our sample
  2. the mean that we observe in our sample
  3. the hypothesised value that we are testing if our sample mean is equal to
  4. (an estimate of) how much we expect samples of our size to vary

Write down or calculate each of these now.

Question 3

Together, the information from the previous question allow us to build up a picture of “what would sample means be like if the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)) were true”.
We can then calculate the probability of seeing a sample mean at least as extreme as the one we have seen, if the null hypothesis were true:

To calculate the probability areas, we’re going to need to get convert our mean into a “test statistic”.

This is called a “Z-statistic”, and it bears a similar resemblance to what we did a few weeks back in calculating “Z-scores”. In this case, however, we are doing the process on the weird abstract theoretical distribution of “the means from all the possible samples we could have taken”.

\[ Z = \frac{M - \mu_0}{SE} \]

Task: Calculate Z

Solution 3. 

zstat <- 
  ( mean(sdata$outlook) - 35 ) / 
    ( sd(sdata$outlook) / sqrt(nrow(sdata)) )

zstat
[1] 1.87

Question 4

Now that we have our test statistic, we can calculate the “p-value”.
This is “probability of seeing a sample mean at least as extreme as the one we have seen, if the null hypothesis were true”, and corresponds to one, or both, of the shaded red areas in Figure 1, depending on how we phrased \(H_1\).

Z statistics are normally distributed with a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1, so we just need to use pnorm() to find the area of the normal distribution curve that is beyond our Z statistic.
