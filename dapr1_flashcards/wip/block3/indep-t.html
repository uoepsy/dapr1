Independent-samples t-test

Example RQ and data

RQ: Do first-generation university students report significantly different mean levels of academic self-efficacy compared to continuing-generation students?

Here’s what the first six rows of the data looks like:

1           2 First         47
2           3 Continuing    44
3           4 Continuing    20
4           5 Continuing    38

6           7 Continuing    33

A higher ASE score represents better academic self-efficacy.

Define hypotheses

The null hypothesis is that there’s no difference between the mean ASE scores of continuing-generation students and first-generation students. Formally:

\[ H_0 : \mu_\text{Continuing} = \mu_\text{First} \]

Based on our research question, our alternative hypothesis is directional: we want to know whether first-generation students have lower ASE than continuing-generation students. Or, flipping these around into alphabetical order (which we’ll need to do in t.test() below), we want to know whether continuing gneeration students have greater ASE than first-generation students. Formally:

\[ H_1 : \mu_\text{Continuing} > \mu_\text{First} \]

To write these hypotheses in plain text in your R script, you can write:

# H0: mu_Continuing = mu_First
# H1: mu_Continuing < mu_First

The useful symbols for your alternative hypothesis are < (less than), > (greater than), and != (not equal to).

Run the test

Use the function t.test(), and give it five pieces of information:

  • the data we want to test, formatted as: <dependent variable> ~ <grouping> (usually we name a column of the data frame using dollar sign notation; see the Subset a dataframe flash card)
  • mu = the difference we would expect between groups if the null hypothesis were true, that is, zero
  • alternative = two.sided, less, or greater; the direction of the \(H_1\)
    • To choose between less or greater, think of the levels of the grouping variable in alphabetical order.
    • For example, the alternative hypothesis \(\mu_B > \mu_A\) must be entered as alternative = 'less', because A (the alphabetically first level) is hypothesised to be less than B (the alphabetically second level).
  • var.equal = TRUE imposes the assumption of equal variance (see below).
  • conf.level = 1 minus our \(\alpha\) level; in Psychology, this will nearly always be .95.
t.test(
  ase_data$ase ~ ase_data$generation,
  # asks: how is ASE different for each level of generation?
  alternative = 'greater',
  mu = 0,
  var.equal = TRUE,
  conf.level = .95
)

Check assumptions and data requirements

Type of requirement / assumption What to check for an independent-samples t-test
Data requirements The dependent variable is numeric.
Assumption of independence The obtained sample data are a random sample from the population of interest, both within and across groups.
Assumption of normality EITHER the population follows a normal distribution
OR the sample size \(\geq\) 30.
Assumption of equal variance The population variance is the same for both groups.

Data requirements

The dependent variable should be interval, ratio, or integer data (that is, numeric).

The ase variable is indeed numeric:

ase_data$ase
 [1] 47 44 20 38 16 33 17 27 38 19 38 24 30 32 10  9 37 47 42  9 54 56

Assumption of independence

The obtained sample data are a random sample from the population of interest, both within and across groups.

This is an issue of study design more than something we can check directly.

But we can still check the data for problems that indicate non-independence between groups:

  • Does each individual who contributed data belong to only one group? (In this case, yes.)
  • Does each individual have only one observation in the group they belong to? (In this case, yes.)
  • Were people allocated to each group at random? (In this case, no!)
    • Even if not: are we willing to assume that people were sampled randomly from the populations of either first-generation or continuing-generation students? (In this case, sure.)

Assumption of normality

EITHER the population for each group follows a normal distribution OR each group’s sample size ≥ 30.

Check sample size

ase_data |>
  group_by(generation) |>
  count()
# A tibble: 2 × 2
# Groups:   generation [2]
  generation     n
  <chr>      <int>
1 Continuing    11
2 First         11

We only have 11 observations per group—way less than 30. So we’ll need to rely on the other checks for normality.

Check normality with a Q-Q plot

The closer the points are to the diagonal line, the better the variable matches an ideal normal distribution.

ase_data |>
  ggplot(aes(sample = ase)) +
  geom_qq() +
  geom_qq_line() +
  facet_wrap(~ generation) +
  labs(
    x = "Theoretical quantiles",
    y = 'Sample quantiles'
  )

Both groups’ data matches the theoretical normal distribution quite well.

Check normality through Shapiro-Wilk test

The Shapiro-Wilk test is a hypothesis test where:

  • \(H_0\): The population data follow a normal distribution
  • \(H_1\): The population data do not follow a normal distribution

We don’t want to reject this H0, so we want a non-significant result!

shapiro.test(ase_data$ase)

    Shapiro-Wilk normality test

data:  ase_data$ase
W = 0.958, p-value = 0.45

Assumption of equal variance

The population variance is the same for both groups.

We test whether the population variances in the two groups are equal using an F-test.

The F-test is a hypothesis test where:

  • \(H_0\): The population variances are equal
  • \(H_1\): The population variances are not equal

We don’t want to reject this H0, so we want a non-significant result!

var.test(
  ase_data$ase ~ ase_data$generation,
  ratio = 1
)

    F test to compare two variances

data:  ase_data$ase by ase_data$generation
F = 1.14, num df = 10, denom df = 10, p-value = 0.84
alternative hypothesis: true ratio of variances is not equal to 1
95 percent confidence interval:

sample estimates:
ratio of variances 
            1.1412

To report this test, you might say:

CautionWhat if I do reject the null hypothesis here?

If you do end up rejecting the H0 here, then in t.test(), change var.equal = TRUE to var.equal = FALSE to run a Welch’s t-test instead. This is a different kind of t-test that relaxes the assumption of equal variance. Otherwise, its interpretation is just the same.

Calculate effect size

For t-tests, the appropriate measure of standardised effect size is Cohen’s D.

To compute this in R, first attach the effectsize package:

library(effectsize)

This package gives us the function cohens_d(). Give it the exact same arguments you’d give t.test() (see above):

cohens_d(
  ase_data$ase ~ ase_data$generation,
  alternative = 'greater',
  mu = 0,
  var.equal = TRUE,
  conf.level = .95
)
Cohen's d |       95% CI
------------------------
0.52      | [-0.20, Inf]

- Estimated using pooled SD.
- One-sided CIs: upper bound fixed at [Inf].

Interpreting Cohen’s D

  • Small or weak effect: around 0.2 (whether positive or negative)
  • Medium or moderate effect: around 0.5 (whether positive or negative)
  • Large or strong effect: around 0.8 or greater (whether positive or negative)

Example write-up of assumptions and results

First, compute some additional descriptive statistics that we’ll need:

ase_data |>
  group_by(generation) |>
  summarise(
    M  = mean(ase),
    SD = sd(ase),
    N  = n()
  )
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  generation     M    SD     N
  <chr>      <dbl> <dbl> <int>
1 Continuing  34.9  14.6    11
2 First       27.5  13.7    11


Now we’re ready to write up all the results together.

We wanted to find out whether first-generation university students report lower Academic Self-Efficacy (ASE) compared to continuing-generation university students. We therefore conducted an independent-samples t-test with \(\alpha\) = .05 to compare ASE scores in these two groups.

ASE scores can range from 9 to 63, and higher scores indicate greater academic self-efficacy. As such, data requirements are met: the dependent variable is numeric. We are also sufficiently satisfied that the assumptions of independence, normality, and equal variance are met. The study design adequately fulfills the assumption of independence. A large sample size (as well as a non-significant Shapiro-Wilk test; W = 0.96, p = .45) suggest normally-distributed data. And based on an F-test for equal variance, we could not we could not reject the null hypothesis of equal variances in the population (F(10, 10) = 1.14, 𝑝 = .84).

We observed a lower mean ASE score for first-generation students (N = 11, M = 27.5, SD = 13.7) than for continuing-generation students (N = 11, M = 34.9, SD = 14.6). However, the t-test indicated no statistically significant difference between the two groups (t(20) = 1.22, p = .12, one-sided). With a Cohen’s D of 0.52, this difference represents a moderate effect.
